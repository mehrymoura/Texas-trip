5.4. History & Curiosities

Bandera (Pop. ~829)

The Claim: "The Cowboy Capital of the World."

The History: In the 1800s, it was a major staging area for the great western cattle trails. Millions of longhorn cattle were driven from here up to the northern markets.

Curiosity: Arkey Blue's Silver Dollar is literally underground (in a basement) and has sawdust on the floor. It's one of the oldest continuously operating honky tonks in Texas. Hank Williams Jr. has played there!

Luckenbach (Pop. ~3)

The Claim: "Everybody's Somebody in Luckenbach."

The History: Established as a trading post in 1849 by German farmers. It became globally famous in 1977 when Waylon Jennings and Willie Nelson released the hit song "Luckenbach, Texas (Back to the basics of love)."

Curiosity: The town consists entirely of a dance hall, a working post office/general store, and a bar. That's it!

Fredericksburg (Pop. ~11,000)

The Claim: The Heart of Texas Wine Country.

The History: Founded in 1846 by German immigrants. They signed one of the only treaties with the Comanche Nation that was never broken.

Curiosity: Look for tiny, narrow houses called "Sunday Houses". German farmers build them to stay in town on weekends when they came in to buy supplies and go to church on Sunday.

Stephenville (Pop. ~21,000)

The Claim: "The other Cowboy Capital of the world."

The History: It's home to Tarleton State University, which has one of the best collegiate rodeo teams in the country. Many professional rodeo cowboys live here because of the training facilities.

Curiosity: Bandera and Stephenville have an ongoing, friendly rivalry over who actually owns the "Cowboy Capital" title.

6.4. History & Curiosities

Weatherford (Pop. ~33,000)

The Claim: "Peach Capital of Texas" and "Cutting Horse Capital of the World."

The History: Once served as the definitive final railhead before travelers plunged directly into the unregulated "Wild West." It was also the home of Mary Martin (the original Broadway Peter Pan) and her son, Larry Hagman (the iconic J.R. Ewing from Dallas).

Curiosity: The historic arena where the Frontier Days Rodeo will be held is operated by the Parker County Sheriff's Posse, one of the oldest, mostly tightly knit, and most prestigious traditional western outfits in the entire state.

Fort Worth (Pop. ~950,000)

The Claim: "Where the West Begins."

The History: While nearby Dallas established itself as a hub for oil, banking, and fine corporate attire, Fort Worth stayed true to its boots. It was the premier staging and trading stop on the historic Chisholm Trail for millions of head of cattle.

Curiosity: Look down while walking the historic district sidewalks—you'll be stepping over the "Texas Trail of Fame", featuring embedded bronze stars honoring true frontier legends.

Jacksboro (Pop. ~4,500)

The Claim: Home of the historic Fort Richardson and the birthplace of the very first state 4-H club back in the 1910s.

The History: First settled in the 1850s as "Mesquiteville", it was a vital stop on the Butterfield Overland Mail stagecoach route. Notably, it was one of the very few Texas counties to vote against secession right before the Civil War.

Curiosity: The town gained massive national attention in 1871 when two famous Kiowa chiefs, Satanta and Big Tree, were brought to the Jacksboro courthouse to face a historic murder trial.

Graham (Pop. ~9,000)

The Claim: Home to the Largest Downtown Square in the United States - a massive layout measuring just over one mile in circumference, originally built wide enough for a full wagon team to completely turn around.

The History: Founded by the Graham brothers in 1872. While searching for salt water a year prior, they accidentally drilled what became the very first natural gas well in the entire state of Texas.

Curiosity: In 1877, under the shade of a massive oak tree in Graham, a hundred local cattlemen gathered to form what is known today as the Texas and Southwestern Cattle Raisers Association to fight rampant frontier cattle theft. Furthermore, nearby Fort Belknap is the only historic fort in Texas fully owned and maintained by the citizens of its own county.

Granbury (Pop. ~11,000)

The Claim: Frequently voted one of the "Best Historic Small Towns in America."

The History: Persistent local legend insists that John Wilkes Booth (Abraham Lincoln's assassin) didn't actually die in a Virginia barn. Instead, folklore claims he fled to Texas, living out a quiet, decades-long life right here under the alias John St. Helen.

Curiosity: Granbury's breathtaking town square was the very first in the state of Texas to be collectively listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

Cleburne (Pop. ~31,000)

The Claim: A massive transportation crossroads right along the Chisholm Trail, later becoming home to the biggest Santa Fe Railroad machine shops west of the Mississippi.

The History: Originally known as Camp Henderson, a temporary outpost where local Civil War soldiers gathered before marching off to battle. It was later incorporated and renamed to honor the famed Confederate General Patrick Cleburne.

Curiosity: In 1886, local farmers met here to draft the "Cleburne Demands" - a legendary, powerful populist document that ignited national labor laws and agricultural protections. Also, if you visit lake Pat Cleburne, you are floating directly over Wardville - the county's original historic seat, which was entirely submerged when they built the lake.

Hico (Pop. ~1,300)

The Claim: "Where Billy the Kid grew old."

The History: History says Pat Garrett shot the famous outlaw Billy the Kid in 1881. However, in 1950, a local man named "Brushy Bill" Roberts claimed he was the real Billy the Kid and had escaped.

Curiosity: The town fully embraces the legend. The Museum is dedicated to proving Brushy Bill was telling the truth!

7.4. History & Curiosities

Alpine (Pop. ~5,800)

The Claim: The Heart of the Big Bend.

The History: Originally named "Murphyville," the town was renamed Alpine in 1887 because of its elevation (4,475 ft) and cool breezes.

Curiosity: When you hike Hancock Hill, look for "The Desk." In 1981, three college students hauled a heavy metal school desk to the top of the mountain so they could study with a view. There is a notebook inside the drawer for hikers to sign!

Fort Davis (Pop. ~1,100)

The Claim: The Highest Town in Texas (Elev. 5,050 ft).

The History: The fort was a major military post established in 1854 to protect emigrants, mail coaches, and freight wagons on the San Antonio - El Paso road.

Curiosity: The McDonald Observatory houses one of the largest telescopes in the world (The Hobby-Eberly Telescope). Because of local light-pollution laws, it has some of the darkest, clearest night skies on the planet.

Marathon (Pop. ~380)

The Claim: The Gateway to Big Bend.

The History: A classic railroad town. The legendary Gage Hotel was built in 1927 by Alfred Gage, a rancher who wanted a comfortable place to stay when he traveled down to check on his massive 500,000-acre cattle empire.

Curiosity: The skies are so clear here that Marathon is officially classified as a "Class 1 Dark Sky" location.

Marfa (Pop. ~1,700)

The Claim: The High Desert Art Capital.

The History: Famous for two things: The 1956 epic Western movie Giant (starring James Dean and Elizabeth Taylor) was filmed here, and in the 1970s, minimalist artist Donald Judd bought an old army base and turned it into a massive art installation.

Curiosity: The Marfa Lights are real, unexplained glowing orbs that dance on the horizon at night. Apaches knew about them, early cowboys wrote about them, and modern scientists still cannot definitively explain what causes them!

Lajitas (Pop. ~50)

The Claim: The Town with the Goat Mayor.

The History: An old quicksilver (mercury) mining outpost and border trading post.

Curiosity: In the 1980s, the town elected a beer-drinking goat named Clay Henry as its Mayor. When the original Clay Henry died, he was stuffed and put in the local trading post. Today, his descendant (Clay Henry IV) continues the legacy in a pen next to the general store - though he prefers water and treats nowadays!

8.4. History & Curiosities

Terlingua (Pop. ~110)

The Claim: The Most Famous Ghost Town in Texas.

The History: In the early 1900s, this was a booming quicksilver (mercury) mining town. When the mines went bust after WWII, everyone abandoned it, leaving homes to crumble. In the 1970s, artists, drifters, and river guides moved into the ruins.

Curiosity: Terlingua is the birthplace of the International Championship Chili Cook-off. Every November, 10,000 people descend on the desert to cook "Texas Red" (Chili with NO Beans—never put beans in Texas chili!).

Big Bend Ranch State Park

The Claim: "The other side of nowhere."

The History: It is the largest state park in Texas (over 300,000 acres). Unlike the National Park, which has paved roads and restaurants, the State Park is intentionally left rugged and wild. It is an old working cattle ranch.

Curiosity: The Sauceda Ranch house is located at the end of a 27 miles unpaved road. Check with rangers if the road is passable.

The Chisos Basin (Big Bend National Park)

The Claim: An "Island in the Sky."

The History: The Chisos Mountains are the only mountain range in the United States fully contained within the boundary of a single National Park.

Juctions / Kerrville

The Claim: The Hill Country Gateway.

The History: You will hit these towns on I-10 on your drive back. They sit on the Llano and Guadalupe Rivers.

Curiosity: Notice the trees! When you hit Junction, you will suddenly see giant, ancient Cypress trees and running rivers again. It's a visual shock after a week in the dusty desert.
